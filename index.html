I build payment platforms for restaurants — and write down how they work.

I work end to end on a UAE restaurant payments platform: the API that moves the money, the panels restaurants run their business from, and the app a guest uses at the table. Most of what I do sits where payments, loyalty and point-of-sale systems meet — which is exactly where the interesting failures live.

This site is where I publish the design documents behind that work: what is being built, how it will behave for every kind of user, and the reasoning behind each decision — written for the people who own the product, not only the people who write the code.

Scope

What I build

Payments

Multi-gateway checkout

Stripe, Telr and Arab Financial Services running side by side, plus Apple Pay, Google Pay and Samsung Pay — and now Tabby for buy-now-pay-later. Split bills, custom amounts, tips, payment links and multi-currency display on top.

Loyalty

Cashback that reconciles

An earn-and-redeem engine with per-branch rules, product-level bonus rates, reward expiry, and claim tokens that let a guest keep the cashback they earned before they had an account.

Integrations

Point-of-sale, joined properly

A two-way Foodics integration for pay-at-table, including a live bill refresh at the exact moment of payment — so a guest never settles a total the kitchen has already changed.

Product surfaces

Three apps, one system

An admin console, a merchant panel with role-based access and reporting, and a guest-facing ordering and payment app. Menus, modifiers, combos, QR codes, table state, dashboards and receipts.

Approach

Money punishes vagueness

The work I am most proud of is the unglamorous kind. A row lock so two people splitting a bill cannot overwrite each other’s payment. An idempotency key so a retried notification never charges twice. Every amount re-derived on the server, so a tampered checkout settles less of the bill rather than clearing it for free.

None of that shows up in a demo. All of it is the difference between a payment system and a payment incident.

Before the code

Read the whole system first

I map how the existing flows actually behave before proposing a change — not how they are assumed to behave. Most integration bugs are already sitting in the assumptions.

During

Design out loud

Decisions get written down with the alternative and the trade-off beside them, so a product owner can push back before anything is built rather than after.

Throughout

Chase the edge cases early

What happens when the customer closes the browser, when two people pay at once, when the bill changes mid-payment. Those cases decide the architecture — they are not cleanup work.
